Constraints by property
1. Concrete strength

* Abrams' law: f_c = A / B^(w/c), with A and B empirical constants. Strength decreases monotonically as w/c rises, and the log of strength is roughly linear in w/c. It holds for fully compacted, same-age, same-materials mixes at roughly w/c 0.3–0.8.
* Powers gel-space ratio: f_c = a·x^n, where x = gel volume / (gel + capillary pore volume). Here x lies in, and n is about 2.6–3.
* Strength-development curves: ACI 209 gives f_c(t) = t/(a + b·t)·f_c28. fib Model Code 2010 gives f_c(t) = exp{s[1 − (28/t)^0.5]}·f_c28. Both rise monotonically with age, are zero at t = 0 (or at the setting time), and approach a finite asymptote. They depend on cement type and curing temperature.
* Further bounds: strength is non-negative, and compaction and air voids lower it.
* References: Neville, Properties of Concrete (Pearson); Mehta & Monteiro, Concrete (McGraw-Hill); fib Model Code 2010 (Ernst & Sohn, 2013).

2. Alloy hardness and strength

* Hall-Petch: σ_y = σ_0 + k·d^(-1/2). Strength rises as grain size falls. It holds above about 10–20 nm. Below that, the inverse Hall-Petch regime caps and then reverses the trend.
* Rule of mixtures: P = Σ x_i·P_i. Voigt (upper) and Reuss (lower) bounds apply to composites.
* Solid-solution strengthening: Labusch gives Δσ ∝ c^(2/3) and Fleischer gives c^(1/2). Varvenne-Curtin gives Δσ_y ∝ f₁(w)·α^(-1/3)·G·((1+ν)/(1−ν))^(4/3)·(Σ c_n ΔV_n²)^(2/3), where the sum is the misfit volume variance. Strength is zero for a pure element (zero variance), and it scales with G and misfit volume to the powers 1/3 and 2/3 respectively. Varvenne-Curtin was derived for random FCC and BCC solid solutions, and it excludes precipitates and short-range order.
* Hardness and strength are related by H ≈ 3σ_y (Tabor). This is approximate and fails for strongly work-hardening or brittle materials.
* Hardness and strength are positive. Strength falls with temperature, and diffusion softens it above about 0.5 T_m.
* References: Hull & Bacon, Introduction to Dislocations; Courtney, Mechanical Behavior of Materials; Varvenne et al., Acta Mater. 118 (2016) 164, [doi:10.1016/j.actamat.2016.07.040](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actamat.2016.07.040) (not verified). A usable HEA application of Varvenne vs Labusch is in [this Cantor-alloy study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1557/s43578-021-00205-6).

3. Polymer Tg

* Flory-Fox: Tg = Tg∞ − K/Mn. Tg rises with molecular weight and saturates at Tg∞. K is about 10⁵ K·g/mol for common polymers. It is valid for linear chains, and for low-Mn oligomers it deviates.
* Fox equation: 1/Tg = w₁/Tg₁ + w₂/Tg₂. For blends and copolymers, Tg lies between the two component values, and it is a weight-fraction harmonic mean. Gordon-Taylor and Couchman-Karasz extend it to non-ideal mixing.
* Further trends: Tg rises with chain stiffness and crosslink density, and falls with plasticizer content and free volume. All values are in kelvin and positive. A plasticizer lowers the Tg of the blend.
* References: Fox & Flory, J. Appl. Phys. 21 (1950) 581; Fox, Bull. Am. Phys. Soc. 1 (1956) 123; Painter & Coleman, Fundamentals of Polymer Science; Rubinstein & Colby, Polymer Physics.

4. Aqueous solubility

* General solubility equation: log S_w = 0.5 − 0.01(MP − 25) − log K_ow. S_w is in mol/L, MP is in °C, and the melting term applies only to solids (set it to 0 for liquids). It has no fitted parameters, and the melting term comes from Clausius-Clapeyron and Walden's rule.
* Reported GSE accuracy on 1026 non-electrolytes: average absolute error 0.38 and RMSE 0.53 log units. Using calculated logP increased RMSD only modestly.
* Limits: it fails for electrolytes and ionisable compounds (pH-dependent), and for strong specific interactions.
* Further trends: solubility falls as logP and MP rise. It is positive and bounded by the pure-liquid miscibility. Temperature dependence follows van't Hoff.
* References: [Jain & Yalkowsky, J. Pharm. Sci. 2001](https://pubs.acs.org/jcisd8/article/41/5/1208/3810549/Prediction-of-Aqueous-Solubility-of-Organic); [Ran et al. dataset summary](https://qsardb.org/repository/handle/10967/188); [Fowles et al. 2025 review](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/93152/13/Fowles-etal-CR-2025-Physics-based-solubility-prediction-for-organic-molecules.pdf).

5. Critical micelle concentration (CMC)

* Klevens: log CMC = A − B·n_C, where n_C is the number of carbons in the alkyl chain. B is about 0.3 for ionics (a factor of 2 per CH₂) and about 0.5 for nonionics (a factor of about 3).
* Corrin-Harkins: log CMC = −a·log(C_i) + b, where C_i is the total counterion concentration. CMC falls as salt rises, with slope about −0.5 to −0.6 for ionic surfactants.
* Further trends: CMC falls with a longer chain, and for ionics it is reduced by added salt. For ionics, CMC shows a shallow U-shape with temperature, with a minimum near 25 °C. For nonionics (cloud-point behaviour), CMC falls as temperature rises. CMC > 0, and it falls as the head group becomes less hydrophilic.
* References: Rosen & Kunjappu, Surfactants and Interfacial Phenomena; Israelachvili, Intermolecular and Surface Forces; Klevens, J. Am. Oil Chem. Soc. 30 (1953) 74; Corrin & Harkins, J. Am. Chem. Soc. 69 (1947) 683.

6. Corrosion rate

* Arrhenius: k = A·exp(−E_a/RT). Rate rises with temperature, with E_a typically 20–60 kJ/mol for activation control. It holds while the mechanism is unchanged. Passive film breakdown or dissolved-gas loss at boiling can reverse the trend.
* Time kinetics: parabolic w² = k_p·t (diffusion-limited, Wagner), x = k·tⁿ (power law), and linear x = k·t (no protective film). The cumulative loss is non-decreasing and starts at zero. The instantaneous rate falls over time for protective films.
* Concentration: rate often has an order dependence r = k·cᵐ. A maximum or a passivation transition is common, for example in acids and chloride.
* References: Jones, Principles and Prevention of Corrosion; Revie & Uhlig, Corrosion and Corrosion Control (Wiley); Kofstad, High-Temperature Corrosion.

7. Ionic conductivity

* Arrhenius: σ·T = A·exp(−E_a/k_B·T). This holds for crystalline solids and in narrow ranges.
* VTF: σ = (A/√T)·exp(−B/(T − T₀)), where T₀ is about Tg − 50 K. It holds for polymer electrolytes, glasses and viscous liquids. It converges to Arrhenius at high T.
* Conductivity is positive and rises with temperature. The composition dependence is typically a maximum, with a rise at low salt due to more carriers, then a fall due to ion pairing and viscosity. Conductivity is bounded by the Nernst-Einstein relation.
* References: Bruce, Solid State Electrochemistry; Angell, Chem. Rev. 2000; Gray, Polymer Electrolytes; Bard & Faulkner, Electrochemical Methods.

8. Crystal properties

* Born stability: the elastic-constant matrix C must be positive definite. For cubic crystals: C₁₁ > |C₁₂|, C₁₁ + 2C₁₂ > 0, and C₄₄ > 0. Hence the bulk modulus K > 0 and shear modulus G > 0. The Voigt-Reuss-Hill bounds and the Pugh ratio follow.
* Thermodynamic stability: formation energy ΔH_f is relative to elemental references, and ΔH_f = 0 for elements. A compound is stable only if its energy above the convex hull is E_hull ≤ 0 (on or below it). The hull is piecewise linear and convex.
* Band gap ≥ 0, and it is zero for metals. DFT-PBE underestimates it.
* Invariances: properties are invariant under rotation, translation and permutation of identical atoms. Tensor properties transform covariantly with the crystal symmetry. Energy is invariant, while forces and stress are equivariant.
* Extensive vs intensive: total energy and volume scale with N. Energy per atom, density and modulus do not. Check the scaling under supercell replication.
* References: Born & Huang, Dynamical Theory of Crystal Lattices; Mouhat & Coudert, Phys. Rev. B 90 (2014) 224104, [doi:10.1103/PhysRevB.90.224104](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.90.224104) (not verified); Sun et al., Sci. Adv. 2 (2016) e1600225.

Studies with reported numbers

* HEA hardness, physics-baseline residual ANN (2026): The model embeds the term G·δ_r^(2/3) and uses residual learning. It reports R² = 0.976 and about 43% lower RMSE than benchmark algorithms. It also shows a monotonic increase of predicted hardness with the theoretical strengthening factor. This is [Hybrid PIML](https://www.oaepublish.com/articles/jmi.2026.16), but it is not an extrapolation test.
* HEA hardness, features from SSS theory: A random forest with theory-guided features reached R² = 0.9416 in 10-fold cross-validation, and 0.8811 for the strengthening part. A separate random forest paper reported a correlation coefficient of 0.91 on an independent test set. These are interpolation numbers from [Acta Phys. Sin.](https://wulixb.iphy.ac.cn/en/article/doi/10.7498/aps.72.20230646) and [this journal article](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2352492823017932).
* Monotonic Gaussian processes (Sandia, 2022): Imposing monotonicity gave a significant reduction in posterior variance. It is strictly monotonic only in the interpolation regime, and the effect fades with distance beyond the training data. It costs a small amount of accuracy against an unconstrained GP. This is a caution for extrapolation claims.
* PINN survey (2025): It states that unconstrained networks can extrapolate poorly, and that physics-inspired activation functions improve extrapolation. For rheology with varying water/cement ratio, RheologyNet had mean relative error 5.8×10⁻⁴ versus 4.2×10⁻³ for a purely data-driven NN. This is [the survey](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11831-025-10448-9).
* Solubility: The GSE alone has RMSE 0.53 log units. However, adding a logP descriptor did not change overall performance for most ML models. So the GSE is a useful baseline to test against, but not a clear source of ML gains.

For your gradient-boosted trees and neural networks, the practical approach is as follows:

* Use `monotone_constraints` in XGBoost or LightGBM for w/c, age, chain length, and 1/√d.
* Transform the target to log or reciprocal space, such as log S, log CMC, and 1/Tg.
* Add the closed-form law as a baseline feature or residual target.
* Add penalty terms for violations of the Born criteria.
* Test the models on held-out ranges, such as unseen ages, chain lengths or temperatures, since interpolation scores do not show the benefit.
